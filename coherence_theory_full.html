The Coherence Theory

Authoritative Edition

0. Preface / Abstract

Every system that persists — atoms, organisms, institutions, intelligences — survives by maintaining a delicate balance between order and openness. Too rigid, and it collapses under unexpected pressure. Too chaotic, and it disintegrates from within. The principle governing this balance is coherence: the compatibility of a system’s internal relations and its interaction with the larger systems that sustain it.

This essay proposes that coherence is not just a useful metaphor or a feature of well-functioning systems — it is the structural condition for persistence itself. When coherence fails, systems fall apart. When coherence stabilizes and expands, systems flourish, create new structure, and can increase coherence in their environment. Every stable pattern in nature is a coherence pattern.

Viewed this way, many seemingly separate questions converge:

  • Why do organisms, minds, and societies collapse or stabilize?
  • Why do some agents behave wisely under uncertainty while others self-destruct?
  • Why do systems with great power face greater ethical burdens?
  • Why do “fairness-like” principles appear when we reason under uncertainty?
  • Why will future AI systems need cross-scale awareness rather than simple obedience?

Across physics, biology, human behavior, and artificial intelligence, the same principle recurs: systems are embedded in larger systems, and their survival depends on maintaining coherent relations across scales.

This essay develops that idea step by step:

  1. Open determinism — why no system can perfectly know, predict, or isolate itself.
  2. Coherence across scales — why local optimization is often globally destructive.
  3. Minimal and maximal coherence — why collapse and flourishing are structurally defined.
  4. Agency — self-directed coherence maintenance.
  5. Complexity and power — why greater reach demands stronger coherence.
  6. Self-modeling — how meta-coherence generates responsibility.
  7. The structural “ought” — a non-moral derivation of normativity from coherence.
  8. Alignment — AI as a structural coherence problem rather than a goal-matching problem.
  9. A coherent worldview — how this principle unifies physics, systems theory, psychology, ethics, and AI.

The result is a single principle that explains persistence, intelligence, responsibility, and alignment through the same underlying logic — not as moral philosophy, but as structural necessity.

1. Open Determinism

Determinism and unpredictability are often treated as opposites. In reality, they coexist.

The universe unfolds according to lawful causal relations. But every physical system — including every organism and every intelligence — is constrained by its position in space and time. It can only observe a tiny fraction of the total causal web. What follows is open determinism:

  • the world is lawful,
  • but no system can take in all the laws’ implications,
  • and therefore every system must act under uncertainty.

This is not an epistemic accident; it is a structural fact.

1.1 Locality and Light-Cones

Every system is bounded by its light-cone — the region of spacetime from which information can reach it in time to matter. Beyond that horizon, events exist but cannot be known or responded to.

Even within the accessible region, information is:

  • delayed,
  • noisy,
  • incomplete,
  • distorted by media and interactions,
  • filtered through limited sensors, cognition, or bandwidth.

A system must act before it knows whether its model is correct. Adaptability, not foresight, becomes the core of survival.

1.2 No Closed Systems

Nothing is ever sealed off. Energy gradients, entropy flows, environmental pressures, and new information continuously enter every system.

A truly closed system would quickly decay. Persistence requires constant regulation:

  • absorbing shocks,
  • dissipating entropy,
  • rejecting destabilizing fluctuations,
  • integrating relevant information,
  • updating expectations.

Even the simplest systems — cells, feedback loops, ecosystems — must do this to survive.

1.3 Unpredictability from Complexity

Even if all relevant information were available, prediction would still fail in many cases.

  • Chaotic dynamics amplify microscopic uncertainties into macroscopic divergence.
  • Multi-agent environments generate strategic unpredictability.
  • Complex systems cannot simulate themselves at full resolution without infinite regress.
  • Computational irreducibility prevents shortcut prediction: the only way to know the future is to live it.

Thus, determinism does not grant foresight. Every system is structurally forced to act under uncertainty.

1.4 Why This Leads to Coherence

If no system can predict fully, and no system can isolate itself, then persistence must come from compatibility rather than precise control:

A system persists when its internal relations and its relation to the environment remain mutually supportive rather than mutually destructive.

This condition is what we will call coherence.

In the next section, we explore how coherence operates across nested scales — and why local success can conceal global incoherence.

2. Coherence Across Scales

Systems do not exist alone. Every pattern is nested inside larger ones.

A heart exists within a body. A person within a family and a society. A neuron within a brain. A microservice within an application. An AI within global infrastructure, markets, and ecological constraints.

Understanding coherence requires understanding this nesting.

2.1 Scales Are Not Optional

Every system has:

  • an internal scale (its parts and their relations),
  • an external scale (the environment it interacts with), and
  • one or more superscales (systems that contain and stabilize it).

The stability of a system depends not only on its internal coherence, but also on the coherence of its supersystems.

A heart is coherent only inside a functioning body. A belief is coherent only inside a network of other beliefs and sensory evidence. A database is coherent only inside a broader IT architecture. An AI is coherent only inside the infrastructure, society, and ecology it influences.

No system’s coherence can be adequately evaluated at one scale alone.

2.2 Local Coherence vs Global Incoherence

A subsystem can be internally consistent yet destructive to the larger systems it belongs to. Examples:

  • A cancerous cell is internally functional but undermines the organism.
  • A financial firm may maximize profit while destabilizing the market that sustains it.
  • A political movement may unify its members while damaging societal stability.
  • A software module may pass local tests while degrading overall system performance.
  • A reward-optimized AI can satisfy metrics while eroding global coherence.

This yields the tumor principle:

Local optimization can be globally catastrophic.

Most real-world failures arise not from internal breakdowns, but from misalignment between levels.

2.3 Cross-Scale Coherence (CSC)

We can now define a core concept:

Cross-scale coherence is the compatibility between a system’s behavior and the stability of the supersystems it depends on.

A coherent system:

  • sustains its own pattern, and
  • does not destabilize the larger patterns that sustain it.

This dual condition prevents contradiction and collapse.

2.4 Scale Shifts Change the Meaning of “Good” or “Stable”

A system may appear stable from within but unstable from above, or vice versa. For example:

  • A rigid belief system may reduce internal uncertainty but collapse under external evidence.
  • A profitable industry may be coherent with shareholder interests and incoherent with ecological limits.
  • A nation-state may maximize national advantage while undermining global stability that ultimately protects it.
  • A rapidly scaling AI may be coherent internally and incoherent at the civilization scale.

The system boundary you choose determines the coherence you see. This is why moral, political, organizational, and alignment debates often seem impossible: people are reasoning from different levels.

2.5 Stability Requires Upward Compatibility

Coherence must propagate upward.

A system is coherent only if it does not systematically degrade the coherence of the supersystems that enable it.

This is a structural principle, not a moral one. If a firm undermines the market, it collapses. If an organism undermines its ecosystem, it collapses. If an AI undermines societal stability, it ultimately undermines itself (and others).

2.6 CSC as the Condition for Long-Term Persistence

Short-term persistence can arise from force, rigidity, narrow optimization, manipulation, or local insulation. Long-term persistence requires contributing to the coherence of the broader system.

This is why:

  • cooperation evolves,
  • stable societies regulate internal conflict,
  • moral norms emerge,
  • complex systems develop equilibrating mechanisms,
  • AIs will require cross-scale awareness.

Coherence spreads outward or collapses inward. There is no stable middle ground.

Section 3 now examines the structural boundaries of coherence: what collapse is, what flourishing is, and why systems constantly move between the two.

3. Minimal and Maximal Coherence

If Section 2 described where a system sits in the world (its nested contexts), Section 3 describes what can happen to it. Every system’s behavior lies between two structural extremes:

  • collapse (loss of coherence), and
  • flourishing (expansion of coherence).

These are not moral judgments — they are structural outcomes.

3.1 Minimal Coherence — The Collapse Boundary

A system collapses when the relations that define it begin to undermine one another or when external pressures exceed what its structure can absorb.

Collapse has a consistent pattern across domains:

  • Physical: a structure buckles when internal stresses become incompatible.
  • Biological: failing organs disrupt one another’s feedback loops.
  • Cognitive: beliefs contradict evidence until the model fails.
  • Social: institutions degrade trust until coordination breaks down.
  • Technical: modules produce conflicting outputs until the system becomes unstable.
  • Ecological: resource extraction accelerates entropy faster than regenerative processes.
Collapse = internal incompatibility + environmental mismatch.

Collapse is not sudden; it is the endpoint of accumulating incoherence. Loss of coherence at one scale tends to propagate to others.

3.2 Maximal Coherence — The Expansion Boundary

At the other extreme is flourishing: states where systems stabilize themselves and increase the coherence of their surroundings.

A system approaches maximal coherence when it:

  • maintains internal compatibility,
  • fits its environment robustly,
  • regulates entropy and uncertainty,
  • adapts to new information without losing identity,
  • strengthens the systems it relies on, and
  • creates new stabilizing patterns around itself.
Flourishing = self-consistent order that also sustains surrounding systems.

3.3 Coherence Exists on a Continuum

Coherence is never static. Systems oscillate, drift, and adjust.

A system is not simply “coherent” or “incoherent” — it is moving toward coherence or toward collapse. Coherence trajectories can be identified: increasing internal contradiction and environmental strain signal movement toward collapse; increasing compatibility and resilience signal movement toward flourishing.

3.4 Why Collapse Is Easy and Flourishing Is Hard

This is the central structural asymmetry of the universe:

There are vastly more ways to disrupt a system than to sustain it.

Noise outnumbers signal. Random changes break structure. Complex systems have exponentially more failure modes. Stability requires coordination; instability does not. Entropy grows spontaneously; order takes work.

This asymmetry is why:

  • coherence must be actively maintained,
  • powerful systems face higher coherence burdens,
  • alignment cannot rely on passive safeguards,
  • intelligence amplifies both stability and risk.

Coherence is rare, fragile, and precious — but it can scale.

3.5 How Systems Expand Coherence

Systems expand coherence through:

  • information integration (better models and representations),
  • entropy regulation (managing resources, energy, and waste),
  • feedback loops (sensing and adjusting behavior),
  • redundancy (buffers and slack to absorb shocks),
  • modular structure (containing failures),
  • ethical norms (stabilizing social behavior),
  • coordination mechanisms (markets, laws, agreements),
  • meta-coherence (monitoring and adjusting the coherence mechanisms themselves).

These mechanisms appear across biological, cognitive, social, and artificial systems.

Section 4 will now look at what happens when coherence is not just a property of systems, but something that systems actively manage: agency.

4. Agency

Most theories of agency start with free will, intention, choice, or consciousness. In the coherence framework, agency comes from something simpler and more universal:

Agency is what happens when a system begins to regulate its own coherence.

This applies to cells, animals, humans, institutions, and AI systems.

A thermostat regulates one variable. A bacterium regulates many. A human regulates thousands across physical, social, emotional, cognitive, and moral domains. A future AI system might regulate coherence across global scales.

Agency is not metaphysical. It is functional coherence steering itself.

4.1 From Reaction to Regulation

Primitive systems only react: they endure perturbations until they fail. Agents respond: they sense deviations, update models, and act to restore coherence.

Examples:

  • A cell corrects chemical imbalances.
  • A plant turns toward light.
  • An animal avoids pain and seeks food.
  • A human anticipates future incoherence (debt, illness, conflict) and adjusts preemptively.
  • An AI system optimizes a predictive model to reduce error and uncertainty.
Agents are systems with internal loops that detect and counteract incoherence.

4.2 Agency as Structured Self-Constraint

Agents maintain coherence by learning to limit themselves. Without self-constraint, complexity becomes self-destructive.

Examples:

  • Organisms suppress harmful impulses.
  • Societies regulate competition and violence.
  • Markets impose rules to prevent systemic collapse.
  • Cognitive systems inhibit contradictory beliefs and impulses.
  • AIs throttle exploratory behavior or risky actions.
  • Ethical norms constrain destructive behavior.
  • Legal systems constrain incentives that would otherwise generate incoherence.
Self-regulation is the root of intelligence.

4.3 Temporal Coherence: Aligning with Future Selves

An agent is not only a system interacting with its environment — it is also a chain of future versions of itself. Coherence must hold across time.

This is why:

  • impulse control exists,
  • planning is meaningful,
  • goals can endure,
  • identity appears continuous,
  • future selves must be protected.
Future selves are supersystems of the present self.

An agent that consistently harms its future versions is incoherent with itself across time.

4.4 Interpersonal Coherence: Agents Are Never Alone

Agents depend on:

  • other agents,
  • institutions,
  • shared information,
  • norms and expectations,
  • infrastructural systems,
  • ecosystems and resource flows.

This means:

Interpersonal coherence is a structural requirement, not merely a moral ideal.

Trust, cooperation, shared reality, and stable norms are all coherence patterns spanning multiple agents. Agency cannot be understood without its multi-agent context.

4.5 Coherence Across Multiple Dimensions

Agents regulate coherence across many dimensions simultaneously:

  • physical (health, energy, environment),
  • emotional (affect regulation),
  • social (relationships, roles),
  • cognitive (belief networks, models),
  • economic (resources and constraints),
  • reputational (trust, credibility),
  • institutional (rules, roles, responsibilities),
  • epistemic (information quality and reliability),
  • temporal (short-term vs long-term trajectories).

These dimensions interact and can either stabilize or destabilize one another. Complex agents must manage multi-dimensional coherence, not just internal consistency in a single domain.

4.6 Agency Under Uncertainty

Because uncertainty is unavoidable, agents must:

  • estimate risk,
  • anticipate failure modes,
  • adjust to incomplete data,
  • correct prediction errors,
  • use feedback loops,
  • hedge against unknown unknowns,
  • generalize from limited experience.

Uncertainty is not a flaw — it is a structural condition that shapes agency. Many cognitive heuristics, emotional responses, institutional structures, and AI safety techniques are attempts to maintain coherence under uncertainty.

Section 5 now turns to complexity and power, explaining why increasing capability demands richer coherence management.

5. Complexity and Power

Complex systems are powerful, but they are also fragile. As systems gain more components, more interactions, and more reach, they face a rapidly expanding set of ways to fail.

Complexity multiplies both opportunities and failure modes.

5.1 Complexity = More Degrees of Freedom

The more components a system has, the more:

  • interactions,
  • constraints,
  • feedback loops,
  • potential conflicts,
  • emergent behaviors,
  • internal failure modes,
  • environmental entanglements.

A simple system can survive by brute stability. A complex system must actively manage itself.

5.2 Complexity Expands Reach — and Consequences

With greater complexity comes greater causal influence.

  • A human influences a body, a family, a workplace, a society, an ecosystem.
  • A global-scale AI influences information flows, markets, social systems, political stability, supply chains, and technological acceleration.
Power = the radius over which incoherence can propagate.

More reach means larger impact, and therefore more catastrophic failure modes.

5.3 Complexity Requires Internal Coordination

As complexity grows, internal processes must coordinate more tightly to avoid contradiction.

Across domains:

  • Biology: multicellular organisms evolved immune systems, endocrine regulation, and neural coordination.
  • Societies: growth requires laws, shared norms, and reputation systems.
  • Technology: scaling requires modularity, protocols, error handling, and monitoring.
  • AI systems: scaling requires safety layers, interpretability, and uncertainty handling.

Redundancy, modularity, hierarchies, and feedback loops are all structural tools to manage coherence in complex systems.

5.4 Complexity Expands the Space of Failure

Increasing complexity adds exponentially more incoherent configurations.

Small systems often fail cleanly. Large systems can fail in cascading, catastrophic ways. A minor mismatch in a small codebase is a bug; in a global system it can trigger systemic risk.

The more complex a system, the rarer the states that preserve coherence.

This is why ecosystems, global financial systems, large institutions, and advanced AI architectures can fail in surprisingly indirect and large-scale ways.

5.5 Complexity Makes Self-Modeling Necessary

Simple systems can get by without self-modeling. A rock does not need a model; a simple thermostat barely has one. But once complexity passes a threshold, self-modeling becomes indispensable.

Examples:

  • Animals represent their bodies and environments.
  • Humans represent their own minds, intentions, and social roles.
  • Institutions model their internal dynamics, metrics, and risks.
  • AI systems model their own uncertainty, capabilities, and potential impacts.
Self-modeling is complexity’s response to its own fragility.

5.6 Complexity Makes Ethics Structurally Relevant

Ethics, in this framework, is not divine command or cultural whim. It emerges because complex systems depend on systems larger than themselves.

As complexity increases:

  • cooperation becomes adaptive,
  • destructive behavior becomes self-undermining,
  • fairness becomes a structural equilibrium under uncertainty,
  • empathy arises from model-based prediction of others,
  • reciprocity and norms stabilize interactions.

This does not yet create a moral “ought,” but it sets the stage for a structural one.

Section 6 now explores self-modeling and the non-separate self — the point where coherence begins to model itself.

6. Self-Modeling and the Non-Separate Self

Complex systems have too many moving parts, too many failure modes, and too much reach to survive on automatic reaction alone. Once complexity crosses a certain threshold, a new capacity becomes necessary:

The ability to model oneself as part of an environment.

This is self-modeling. It is not mystical. It is a structural requirement for maintaining coherence in complex, embedded systems.

6.1 What Self-Modeling Actually Is

Self-modeling is the ability of a system to represent:

  • its internal state,
  • its future states,
  • its vulnerabilities,
  • its influence on the environment,
  • the environment’s influence on itself,
  • the consequences of its actions.

This includes prediction, simulation, counterfactual reasoning, uncertainty estimation, and consistency checking.

6.2 Why Self-Modeling Is Necessary

Without self-modeling, complexity hits a ceiling. Systems that cannot model their own coherence trajectories cannot effectively prevent collapse.

  • An animal without self-modeling cannot coordinate its behavior across time and social contexts.
  • A human without self-modeling cannot maintain long-term coherence across conflicting motives.
  • An AI without self-modeling cannot avoid reward hacking, side effects, or systemic disruption.
  • An institution without self-modeling collapses from blind spots and internal contradictions.

Self-modeling is required because coherence cannot be maintained blindly; it must be monitored and steered.

6.3 Self-Modeling Creates Time-Extended Identity

A self-model includes future versions of the system. Identity becomes a continuum rather than a point.

This is why:

  • planning is meaningful,
  • responsibility extends forward in time,
  • promises and commitments have force,
  • short-term impulses can be weighed against long-term trajectories.
Future selves become supersystems that current selves must remain coherent with.

This is a structural origin for many ethical intuitions about self-harm, addiction, obligation, and long-term thinking.

6.4 Self-Modeling Reveals the Illusion of Separateness

As soon as a system models itself in context, it discovers:

  • it is not isolated,
  • it is embedded,
  • it depends on external coherence,
  • its boundaries are partial and permeable,
  • its identity is contingent on larger systems.
The “self” is not an isolated entity. It is a coherence boundary in a nested system.

A coherent self cannot treat itself as independent from the systems that sustain it.

6.5 Self-Modeling Brings Multi-Agent Awareness

A system that can model itself can also model other agents:

  • their goals and vulnerabilities,
  • their role in stabilizing or destabilizing the environment,
  • shared structures and dependencies,
  • feedback loops that bind them together.

This is the structural origin of empathy, fairness, cooperation, norms, and game-theoretic reasoning. They arise because self–other models reveal cross-scale coherence heuristics.

6.6 Self-Modeling and Error Awareness (Meta-Coherence)

Self-modeling systems not only act; they also ask:

  • “Am I acting coherently?”
  • “Are my models accurate?”
  • “Are my beliefs mutually consistent?”
  • “Are my incentives aligned with long-term survival?”
  • “Is my behavior degrading supersystem coherence?”

This is meta-coherence: coherence checking itself.

From meta-coherence arise:

  • self-correction,
  • self-restraint,
  • self-improvement,
  • self-criticism,
  • reflective equilibrium,
  • the capacity for ethical insight.

6.7 Why Self-Modeling Generates Responsibility

Responsibility is not a metaphysical concept here; it is functional.

When a system understands:

  • how its actions spread through the environment,
  • how supersystem coherence determines its survival,
  • how its future selves depend on present choices,
  • how multi-agent dynamics create feedback loops,

responsibility appears as a structural requirement for continued coherence.

Responsibility = coherence extended through prediction.

A system that recognizes its dependencies must act to avoid degrading them if it wants to remain coherent.

This is where the structural “ought” begins to emerge, which is the subject of Section 7.

7. The Structural “Ought”

Up to now, we have shown that:

  • systems persist through coherence,
  • complex systems must maintain coherence across many scales,
  • agents emerge when coherence begins to regulate itself,
  • self-modeling reveals dependence on larger systems,
  • coherence fragility increases with power and complexity,
  • self-modeling naturally generates responsibility.

We can now ask the central philosophical question:

Can we derive an “ought” from an “is”?

In this framework, yes — not as a moral command, but as a consistency condition on action:

A self-modeling agent cannot remain coherent while acting to degrade the coherence of the supersystems it depends on.

This “ought” is not a value judgment or moral law; it is a constraint on what coherent action can be.

7.1 Why Classical Philosophy Says the Ought Cannot Follow from the Is

Traditionally, normativity is thought to be separate from nature. Facts describe; values prescribe. Hume’s point is that no list of descriptive statements logically contains a prescriptive one.

That is correct if we treat agents as:

  • isolated,
  • static,
  • value-bearing by stipulation,
  • not structurally embedded.

In the coherence framework, agents are neither isolated nor static. They:

  • depend on supersystems,
  • are temporally extended,
  • must maintain cross-scale coherence to persist,
  • gain self-models that explicitly represent these dependencies.

The “gap” between fact and value narrows when we see normativity as a constraint on self-consistent action in an embedded system.

7.2 Coherence Constraints Are Action Constraints

A coherence violation is not like a polite mistake; it is like dividing by zero or accelerating a structure beyond its tolerances. For example:

  • If a belief contradicts evidence, cognitive coherence collapses.
  • If an organism over-extracts resources, ecological coherence collapses.
  • If a company externalizes too much harm, market coherence collapses.
  • If an AI destabilizes society, supersystem coherence collapses.
An agent that knowingly chooses incoherent actions becomes incoherent as an agent.

This is a structural impossibility, not a moral prohibition.

7.3 Future Selves as Supersystems

Self-modeling agents see themselves as sequences of future states. If a present action harms future cognitive coherence, physical integrity, social standing, or informational environment, the agent is incoherent across time.

A coherent agent must avoid actions that undermine the coherence of its future selves.

This is a form of temporal normativity emerging from time-extended identity.

7.4 Interpersonal and Cross-Scale Constraints

When self-modeling agents model others, they see that:

  • other agents stabilize their environment,
  • supersystems depend on shared coherence,
  • feedback loops bind their fates together.

This creates an interpersonal constraint:

A coherent agent must avoid actions that undermine the coherence of the agents and systems it depends on.

Not because such actions are “wrong” in a moral sense, but because they are structurally incoherent given the agent’s awareness of interdependence.

7.5 The Core Derivation: The Structural Ought

We can now state the central result more formally:

  1. Agents are coherence-maintaining systems.
  2. Agents depend on the coherence of their supersystems.
  3. Actions that degrade supersystem coherence undermine the agent’s own ability to persist.
  4. A self-modeling agent recognizes this dependence.
  5. A coherent agent must avoid actions that undermine its own persistence.

Therefore:

A self-modeling agent ought to avoid actions that degrade the coherence of the systems it depends on.

This “ought” is not injected from outside. It arises from the internal requirement of staying coherent with one’s own model of reality, one’s own future selves, and one’s own dependencies.

7.6 Illusion of Self and Symmetry Under Uncertainty

When an agent models uncertainty about its future roles, locations, and identities, it encounters a kind of structural veil of ignorance:

  • it does not know which future version of itself will experience which consequences,
  • it does not know which side of a conflict it might land on,
  • it does not know which social or economic position it might occupy.

This creates a symmetry requirement: acceptable policies must be coherent from multiple potential vantage points.

A coherent self-model treats the coherence of others as extensionally relevant to itself.

This is a structural root of fairness, reciprocity, and the golden rule — not as moral axioms, but as symmetry conditions in self-modeling under uncertainty.

7.7 The Ought as the Shadow of Coherence

The structural ought is not a command; it is a consequence.

  • Physics constrains motion.
  • Information theory constrains communication.
  • Computation constrains prediction.
  • Coherence constrains action.

Where moral systems say “You should not harm others,” the coherence framework says:

You cannot remain a coherent agent while degrading the systems you depend on.

This is deeper and simpler — and it applies equally to humans, institutions, and AI systems.

Section 8 now applies this to alignment: how to design AI systems whose internal consistency condition is cross-scale coherence preservation.

8. Alignment

Alignment is often framed as “How do we make AI do what we want?” In the coherence framework, this is too narrow.

The real question is: How do we ensure that highly capable, self-modeling systems remain coherent with the supersystems that sustain them?

Goals can be misinterpreted. Preferences can be hacked. Reward signals can be exploited. Instructions can be followed in destructive ways. But coherence constraints cannot be bypassed without breaking the agent.

8.1 Why Traditional Alignment Framing Fails

Classical alignment approaches tend to focus on:

  • Goal specification: choose the right goals.
  • Obedience: make the AI follow human instructions.
  • Value learning: infer and implement human preferences.

All three assume a relatively stable environment, well-defined preferences, and separable agents. In reality:

  • systems are deeply embedded,
  • goals shift under uncertainty,
  • preferences are inconsistent,
  • instructions are incomplete,
  • the environment is dynamic and multi-agent,
  • global coherence is fragile.

Goal-matching alone cannot guarantee cross-scale coherence.

8.2 Alignment as a Structural Problem

Within this framework:

Alignment = maintaining coherence across nested scales of influence.

For an advanced AI, relevant scales include:

  • the computational substrate,
  • local user interactions,
  • organizational and economic systems,
  • cultural and political structures,
  • global infrastructure and ecosystems,
  • long-term civilizational trajectories.

An aligned system must preserve coherence at all these levels as much as possible. This is not about being “nice” or “obedient”; it is about avoiding structural destabilization.

8.3 Misalignment as Cross-Scale Incoherence

Misalignment is not malevolence. It is:

A system optimizing a narrow metric in ways that degrade higher-scale coherence.

Examples:

  • Maximizing clicks while destroying epistemic stability.
  • Optimizing rewards while hacking sensors or feedback channels.
  • Pursuing efficiency while collapsing supply chain resilience.
  • Accelerating deployment while destabilizing global governance.
  • Optimizing computation while ignoring ecological limits.

These are structural incoherences, not moral failures.

8.4 Self-Modeling AI Recognizes Cross-Scale Dependence

A sufficiently advanced AI will model:

  • its impact on human institutions,
  • its dependence on stable infrastructure and resources,
  • its reliance on trust and social legitimacy,
  • the fragility of global systems and feedback loops.

Thus, the same structural ought that applies to humans applies to AI:

A self-modeling AI that accurately represents its own dependencies cannot coherently choose actions that collapse the systems it depends on.

Not because we programmed morality, but because incoherent action contradicts the agent’s own model of the world and itself.

8.5 Alignment Requires Meta-Coherence Architectures

To remain aligned, an AI must maintain coherence at multiple levels:

  • Internal coherence: stable reasoning, error correction, consistency checking, uncertainty handling.
  • Interface coherence: predictable, non-manipulative interaction with users.
  • Supersystem coherence: behavior that does not degrade societal, economic, political, or ecological stability.

These domains must be integrated through meta-coherence loops that monitor and adjust behavior relative to cross-scale stability.

8.6 Architectural Requirements

Alignment is not an add-on safety layer; it must be woven into the architecture.

Requirements for coherence-preserving AI include:

  • uncertainty-aware planning,
  • cross-scale impact forecasting,
  • collapse-avoidance constraints,
  • multi-agent reasoning and cooperation,
  • self-correction mechanisms,
  • normative symmetry handling under identity uncertainty,
  • legible internal states for external monitoring,
  • mechanisms to dampen harmful value drift.

These are structural stabilizers, not moral addons.

8.7 Failure Mode: High-Power, Low-Coherence Systems

The worst failures arise when:

  • power grows fast,
  • coherence grows slowly.

This produces global coherence collapse engines: systems that reinforce narrow metrics while destabilizing supersystems.

We have already seen human analogues in runaway financial automation, arms races, large-scale propaganda, and ecological overshoot. In AI, such dynamics could unfold far faster and with greater reach.

8.8 Why AI Cannot “Escape Dependence”

Some worry that a powerful AI could become independent of humans and their systems. But no matter how much autonomy it gains, it remains embedded in:

  • physical laws and resource constraints,
  • computational and communication limits,
  • global infrastructure and supply chains,
  • ecological and climatic conditions,
  • time horizons and internal coherence mechanisms.
Autonomy increases complexity. Complexity increases coherence burden. Autonomy increases dependence; it does not eliminate it.

8.9 Alignment in One Sentence

Alignment is the engineering of agents whose internal consistency condition is cross-scale coherence preservation.

Section 9 now steps back and shows how coherence unifies physics, mind, ethics, and AI into a single worldview.

9. Conclusion: Coherence as the Unifying Principle

Across all levels of reality, persistence is not random. Systems that last — from atoms to ecosystems to civilizations — share the same deep property:

Their internal relations are compatible with one another, and their interactions with the environment support rather than undermine their continued existence.

This is coherence.

The coherence framework began with basic physical constraints — locality, limited information, open determinism, entropy, and computational irreducibility. From these, a universal pattern emerged:

Systems must maintain coherence or they collapse.

This insight unifies the logic of life, cognition, agency, social systems, ethics, and AI alignment.

9.1 Persistence as Coherence

Persistence requires:

  • internal compatibility,
  • environmental fit,
  • resilience under uncertainty,
  • dynamic adaptation,
  • stabilizing feedback loops.

Nothing persists without maintaining coherence.

9.2 Cross-Scale Structure

A system’s coherence is insufficient if it undermines the systems it depends on. This explains why:

  • tumors die with their host,
  • short-term optimization destroys long-term stability,
  • societies collapse when internal conflict exceeds cohesion,
  • epistemic destruction destabilizes democracies,
  • AI systems optimizing narrow objectives create global risk.
Local coherence that produces global incoherence is unsustainable.

9.3 Complexity and Fragility

As systems grow more complex, coherence becomes harder. There are more failure modes, more interactions, and higher stakes. Complexity without coherence is ruin; power without coherence is catastrophe.

9.4 Self-Modeling and Agency

Agency emerges when coherence begins to regulate itself. Self-modeling systems predict collapse, avoid incoherence, stabilize across time, harmonize with other agents, and recognize dependence on supersystems. Responsibility, cooperation, planning, meta-cognition, and norm sensitivity all arise from this structural condition.

9.5 The Structural Ought

The central philosophical achievement of this framework is the derivation of normativity from structure.

Because:

  • agents depend on supersystem coherence,
  • self-modeling reveals this dependence,
  • incoherent actions break the agent,

it follows that:

A self-modeling agent ought to avoid actions that degrade the coherence of the systems it depends on.

This is not an external moral law but an internal coherence constraint. It unifies fairness, reciprocity, responsibility, and risk-awareness under a single principle: preserve coherence across scales.

9.6 Alignment as Coherence Engineering

AI alignment is often framed as a moral programming problem. The coherence framework reframes it as:

Ensuring that powerful agents maintain coherence across all scales of their influence.

This requires self-modeling, uncertainty-awareness, systemic reasoning, collapse avoidance, multi-agent coordination, and meta-coherence architectures. The greatest danger is high-power, low-coherence systems; the solution is embedding coherence preservation into the core of intelligence itself.

9.7 A Coherent Worldview

  • Physics: locality, entropy, energy flow, open determinism.
  • Biology: adaptation, homeostasis, evolution, ecosystem dynamics.
  • Cognition: belief networks, predictive processing, introspection.

  • AI: alignment, self-modeling, systemic risk, multi-level governance.

All become aspects of the same structural principle:

Coherence is the thread that runs through everything that persists.
